I am a Ph.D. Candidate in Operations Management & Business Analytics at the [Fisher College of Business](https://fisher.osu.edu/), The Ohio State University, and a candidate for tenure-track faculty positions in the **2026-2027 job market**.

My research examines how powerful supply chain intermediaries and their contracts shape supply chain performance, with a focus on the retail pharmaceutical supply chain, payer/insurance contracts, and pro-environmental operations. I use a broad empirical toolkit — applied econometrics, causal inference, and field and laboratory experiments — to produce evidence useful to managers and policymakers. Before beginning my Ph.D., I spent 12 years at Accenture working across IT operations and business analytics in healthcare, insurance, and technology.

Research Interests
======
* **Topics:** Supply chain intermediaries, third-party payment contracts, pro-environmental operations through AI, behavioral operations
* **Contexts:** Retail pharmaceutical supply chain; payer/insurance contracts; household recycling
* **Methodology:** Applied econometrics, causal inference (difference-in-differences, synthetic controls, matching, decomposition, causal machine learning), field experiments, lab experiments

Job Market Paper
======
### Regulating Powerful Intermediaries: An Empirical Assessment in the Retail Pharmacy Supply Chain
**Nikhil Sharma, John Gray, Jurriaan De Jong** &nbsp;|&nbsp; *Manuscript ready for submission, M&SOM*

I examine regulatory actions taken by Ohio Medicaid targeting spread pricing — a contentious practice in which a Pharmacy Benefit Manager (PBM) bills the payer more for a drug than it reimburses the dispensing pharmacy, retaining the difference. Ohio Medicaid first introduced a payment transparency audit in 2018 and subsequently banned spread pricing in 2019. Using a difference-in-differences design on a coarsened exact-matched sample, complemented by an event-study analysis using synthetic controls, I find that (i) payment transparency reduced Medicaid payer costs and lowered drug-quality risk, (ii) the subsequent spread-pricing ban reversed a substantial portion of the initial cost savings while the overall quality improvement persisted, and (iii) these effects varied across drugs. The findings offer practical insights for other states considering similar regulation of PBM practices.

*Presented at POMS 2025, DSI 2025, POMS 2026, and ISA 2026. Awarded First Place, Hayes Graduate Research Forum 2025 (Business category), The Ohio State University.*
