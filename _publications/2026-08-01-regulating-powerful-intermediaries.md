**Job Market Paper**

I examine regulatory actions taken by Ohio Medicaid targeting one of the most contentious Pharmacy Benefit Manager (PBM) practices, spread pricing, in which a PBM bills the payer more for a drug than it reimburses the dispensing retailer, retaining the difference. This is an understudied area in the supply chain intermediary literature, where an intermediary that does not take physical title to the product extracts compensation not visible to its trading partners. Ohio Medicaid first introduced a payment transparency audit in 2018 and subsequently banned spread pricing in 2019.

I examine whether these regulatory actions on payment transparency and the spread-pricing ban improved supply chain outcomes — specifically payer costs and drug-quality risk — in the pharmaceutical supply chain. Leveraging the two policies as exogenous events, I use a difference-in-differences design on a coarsened exact-matched sample, complemented by an event-study analysis using synthetic controls. I find that (i) payment transparency led to reduced Medicaid payer costs and lowered drug-quality risk, (ii) the subsequent spread-pricing ban reversed a substantial portion of the initial cost savings while the overall quality improvement persisted, and (iii) these effects varied across drugs, with the cost reversal and changes in quality risk occurring among different product groups.

The findings offer practical insights for other states considering regulation of PBM spread pricing.
